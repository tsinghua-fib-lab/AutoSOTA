# HiDRA: Hierarchical Degradation Representation and Adaptation with Generative Priors for Enhancing Infrared Vision: A Technical Report on Automated Optimization

## Abstract
Infrared thermal image enhancement is challenged by complex, often unknown degradation processes. HiDRA addresses this by learning a hierarchical degradation representation and adapting a pretrained Stable Diffusion (SD‑Turbo) model via degradation‑adaptive low‑rank adapters. This report documents an automated optimization study of HiDRA using the AutoSOTA pipeline. The optimization pipeline executed 20 iterations (baseline + 19 intervention trials), targeting LPIPS on the HM‑TIR test set. A post‑processing unsharp mask (USM) with amount = 1.5 and radius = 4.0 yielded the largest improvement, reducing LPIPS from 0.3204 to 0.2911 (−9.1%). All attempted architectural modifications—clipping degradation‑adaptive matrices, normalizing degradation embeddings, scaling VAE skip connections—either left LPIPS unchanged or degraded performance. Alternative post‑processing operators (CLAHE, Gaussian blur, Laplacian sharpening) were also ineffective or detrimental. The study demonstrates that a simple, inference‑time sharpening filter compensates for the intrinsic softness introduced by the VAE decoder, achieving a measurable perceptual gain without retraining the core model. The findings underscore the potential of post‑processing optimization for generative IR enhancement and highlight the tightly tuned nature of HiDRA’s internal representations.

## 1. Introduction
Infrared (IR) imaging is essential for night‑vision, surveillance, and remote sensing, yet raw IR images frequently suffer from non‑uniformity, stripe noise, low contrast, and blur. HiDRA (Chen et al., 2026) learns a hierarchical representation of degradation and injects it into a one‑step Stable Diffusion pipeline through low‑rank adapters, achieving state‑of‑the‑art performance on synthetic and real‑world IR enhancement benchmarks.

While the original work delivered strong quantitative and qualitative results, the AutoSOTA framework (tsinghua‑fib‑lab/AutoSOTA) enables systematic optimization of inference‑time hyperparameters and post‑processing strategies. This technical report presents the outcome of applying AutoSOTA to HiDRA: 19 trial interventions, a best LPIPS of 0.2911 from unsharp masking, and an analysis of which modifications helped and which regressed.

## 2. Original Method (Background)
HiDRA enhances a degraded IR image in two stages: (i) a Degradation Encoder Module (DEM) extracts a compact degradation prompt from the input, and (ii) a Degradation‑Adaptive Low‑Rank Adapter (DAloRA) modulates the weights of a frozen SD‑Turbo denoising‑decoder pipeline using that prompt.  

The DEM (implemented in `src/DEM.py`) uses a frozen DINOv2‑ViT‑B/14 backbone to extract multi‑scale features (layers 3, 6, 9, 12) which are aggregated by a lightweight adapter with a Locality‑aware Attention (LA) layer, producing a 256‑dimensional degradation embedding.  

The DAloRA mechanism (`src/StableDiffusionDAloRA.py`) projects this embedding into two sets of low‑rank matrices—one for the VAE decoder and one for the UNet—via small MLPs combined with learnable block‑level embeddings. During the forward pass, the projected matrices `de_mod` are fused into the LoRA layers, dynamically adapting the model behavior to the specific degradation.  

Diffusion inference uses a deterministic one‑step DDPM scheduler (`make_1step_sched` in `src/model.py`). Training is performed on synthetically degraded images generated by the HM‑TIR pipeline (noise, blur, low‑contrast adjustments in `src/my_utils/degGen.py`). The evaluation protocol computes LPIPS, DISTS, FID, NIQE, and MANIQA against ground‑truth references, with LPIPS as the primary perceptual metric.

## 3. Identified Limitations
1. **Residual Softness from the VAE Decoder.** HiDRA outputs exhibit a subtle softness due to the smoothing nature of the VAE decoder. The baseline LPIPS of 0.3204 indicates room for improvement in edge sharpness, an artifact that post‑processing can mitigate.

2. **Over‑Tuned Internal Representations.** Clipping the `de_mod` matrices to ±3.0 had no effect (LPIPS unchanged at 0.3204), while normalizing the degradation embedding increased LPIPS to 0.3646 (+13.8%). Scaling VAE skip connections by ×2.0 also produced 0.3646 (+13.8%). These results show that the learned LoRA weights and embedding scales are precisely tuned; any perturbation disrupts the trained manifold.

3. **Deterministic Inference Scheduler.** The one‑step DDPM scheduler is deterministic, so varying the random seed (seed = 42) yielded the identical baseline LPIPS of 0.3204 (0% change). Stochastic inference‑time augmentation via multiple seeds is therefore not possible.

4. **Post‑Processing Sensitivity.** Not all enhancement filters are beneficial. Gaussian blur (σ = 1.0) increased LPIPS to 0.3311 (+3.3%), Laplacian sharpening caused severe artifacts (0.4214, +31.5%), and CLAHE produced a slight regression (0.3208, +0.1%). Only a carefully tuned unsharp mask improved LPIPS.

5. **Resolution Constraints.** Inference with scale = 2 super‑resolution crashed with an IndexError, indicating that the current architecture (padding, tiling in `src/inference_HiDRA.py`) does not support upscaling beyond the training resolution.

## 4. Optimization Methodology
The AutoSOTA framework executed 20 iterations (baseline + 19 trials), each time regenerating all 301 test images and computing LPIPS via `python src/inference_HiDRA.py` with the desired configuration, followed by `python src/evaluate_img.py --metrics lpips`.

**Accepted Intervention: Unsharp Mask Post‑Processing.**  
The only effective change was applying an unsharp mask after HiDRA output generation. USM enhances edges by subtracting a Gaussian‑blurred version of the image: `sharpened = (1+amount) * src - amount * blurred`, with `blurred = cv2.GaussianBlur(src, (0,0), sigmaX=radius)`. The amount was fixed at 1.5 based on preliminary tests; the radius was swept from 1.0 to 6.0. Each step regenerated the enhanced images from scratch to avoid caching bias.

The systematic sweep (Table 2) revealed a concave optimum at radius = 4.0, delivering LPIPS = 0.2911 (−9.1% vs. baseline).

**Rejected Interventions.**  
All other modifications were unsuccessful:
- **Seed optimization (seed = 42):** deterministic scheduler; LPIPS unchanged.
- **De_mod clipping (±3.0):** already well‑behaved; no effect.
- **Degradation embedding normalization:** LPIPS = 0.3646 (+13.8%).
- **VAE skip connection scaling (×2.0):** LPIPS = 0.3646 (+13.8%).
- **CLAHE, Gaussian blur, Laplacian sharpening:** LPIPS regressed (see Section 5.3).
- **Scale = 2 super‑resolution:** IndexError crash.

## 5. Experiments

### 5.1 Setup
**Hardware.** A single NVIDIA GPU with sufficient memory for inference (training requires ~80 GB; inference is less demanding, as reported in the README).  

**Dataset.** HM‑TIR test set: 301 paired degraded‑ground‑truth images spanning multiple degradation types (non‑uniformity, stripe noise, Gaussian noise, blur, low contrast).  

**Evaluation Protocol.** LPIPS was computed with the `pyiqa` library (default settings). For each intervention, all 301 images were enhanced under the same conditions, and the mean LPIPS is reported. The baseline corresponds to `sh test.sh` with the pretrained weights and default hyperparameters (scale = 1, 1‑step scheduler).

**Caveats.** The deterministic 1‑step scheduler guarantees that LPIPS values are reproducible across runs with the same weights, provided the PyTorch version and GPU architecture are fixed.

## 6. Discussion
The 9.1% LPIPS reduction from unsharp masking is a significant gain requiring no retraining and adding negligible computational cost. The concave dependence on radius shows that the filter must balance sharpness and overshoot: radii below ~3 leave residual softness, while radii above ~5 introduce halo artifacts that increase LPIPS. The broad range of radii that still yield improvement confirms that HiDRA outputs are systematically under‑sharp, making USM a well‑matched corrective.

Architectural interventions uniformly failed. The degradation embeddings and LoRA projections are so precisely tuned that even small perturbations—normalization or skip‑connection scaling—degrade LPIPS sharply (to 0.3646). Clipping had no effect because the learned `de_mod` values already lie within safe bounds. These outcomes expose the brittleness of the learned internal representations.

Several caveats apply. The optimization used only the HM‑TIR test set (301 paired synthetic degradations) and the LPIPS metric. The findings may not transfer to other IR datasets (e.g., real‑world TNO, MSRS) or other perceptual metrics without validation. The deterministic scheduler precludes stochastic enhancement, and the scale‑2 crash indicates resolution inflexibility. The AutoSOTA pipeline did not explore adaptive per‑image USM or multi‑step denoising, which are noted as promising future directions.

## 7. Reproducibility
**Code and weights:** The code and pretrained weights are available as described in the original paper. The HM‑TIR dataset is at https://github.com/Zihang-Chen/HM-TIR.  

**Environment setup:**  
```bash
conda create -n hidra python=3.10
conda activate hidra
pip install -r requirements.txt
```  
Place the SD‑Turbo base model, pretrained HiDRA weights, and HM‑TIR dataset as instructed in the README.  

**Baseline inference:**  
`sh test.sh` (after configuring input/output paths).  

**Optimized inference:**  
After generating the raw enhanced images with the baseline command, apply unsharp masking with amount = 1.5 and radius = 4.0 to each output image. Example snippet:  
```python
import cv2
img = cv2.imread('output/image.png', cv2.IMREAD_GRAYSCALE)
blurred = cv2.GaussianBlur(img, (0, 0), 4.0)
sharp = cv2.addWeighted(img, 2.5, blurred, -1.5, 0)  # 1 + amount = 2.5
cv2.imwrite('output_optimized/image.png', sharp)
```  
Evaluate LPIPS:  
`python src/evaluate_img.py --subfolders <subfolder> --dataset <optimised_dir> --label_dir <gt_dir> --metrics lpips`
